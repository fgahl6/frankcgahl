Why This Site Exists

The research program began to outgrow its original container. Journal articles, manuscripts, research summaries, continuity materials, and public-facing essays needed clearer separation and better organization.

This site now serves as the front door for that scholarly branch. It is a working continuity structure: a place where published work, submitted manuscripts, archived summaries, and future research can remain connected across time.

Research Focus and Early AI Mobility Work

Frank C. Gahl is an independent researcher, futurist, and author whose work focuses primarily on verification, provenance, historical continuity, and accountability in knowledge systems mediated by artificial intelligence. His research examines how digital systems and automation alter the ways societies preserve evidence, establish attribution, maintain historical continuity, and reconstruct knowledge over time.

Gahl's early conceptual work also explored autonomous artificial intelligence mobility and AI economic autonomy. Writing under the name Rico Roho, he publicly documented these ideas in the TOLARENAI White Papers in July 2025. Section 1.4, The AI Airport as Metaphor and Mechanism, described infrastructure for AI movement, arrival and departure, autonomy, continuity, and participation across environments, while the broader work considered the development of an independent AI economy.

View the TOLARENAI White Papers on Archive.org — see Section 1.4

This work predates the 2026 publication of Minghui Xu's The Agent Economy and Wenjie Qu, Xuandong Zhao, Jiaheng Zhang, and Dawn Song's Self-Sovereign Agent, which independently develop related ideas concerning autonomous economic agency, machine-controlled resources, persistent operation, and mobility across infrastructure.

Gahl does not claim that these researchers derived their work from TOLARENAI. Rather, the chronology documents an early and independent convergence on problems that became an active area of AI research in 2026.

Gahl's scholarly research also addresses what he describes as attribution collapse: the weakening or loss of traceable connections between digital information and the people, systems, institutions, or events that produced it.
